\subsubsection{Search and Screening Details.}
The paragraphs below give the details that Section~3 of the paper leaves out.

\emph{Design and reporting.}
The items of PRISMA 2020 on risk of bias, effect measures, synthesis and certainty of evidence do not apply to a mapping of methods.
The search is reported following PRISMA-S.
% Removed: these files are not available in this repository or elsewhere yet.
%The review plan, the instructions given to the screening models, the register and the validation files are deposited.

\emph{Databases and queries.}
OpenAlex indexes the proceedings of IEEE, ACM, Springer, Elsevier and CVF.
Semantic Scholar added abstracts that OpenAlex lacked.
Europe PMC covers the biomedical and ergonomics literature.
The 158 queries ran in four passes of 50, 52, 34 and 22 queries, listed below.
The first pass covered measuring distances and angles between objects named in words.
The second covered locating a named point on an object.
The third covered models that output the 3D position of a point given by a phrase or an example image.
The fourth added vocabulary of model outputs, because the first three passes had missed whole families of recent models.
What a model outputs is often stated only in the full text or on its card, for example as ``3D waypoints'', ``depth tokens'' or ``region tokens''.

The second round took the 14 such methods that the first round had added.
Their 890 references gave 448 new records, but no new method of this kind, so the backward search was stopped.
Semantic Scholar has no reference list for 18 of these methods, mostly model cards and recent preprints.

\emph{Screening.}
Two of the 15\,559 unique records had no title and were not screened.
At stage~1, Kimi K2.6 was queried through OpenRouter by a script.
The provider was fixed to unquantised weights, the temperature was 0, and each record was one call with the screening instruction of the review plan verbatim.
Stage~1 could assign only reasons decidable from an abstract.
Reasons that depend on a method section (a lifted pixel, unnamed points, a given set of points) were left to stage~2.
Stage~1 relies on abstracts alone, so 85 of the excluded records were assessed in full text at stage~2.
They were the records that a first check had flagged, candidates known to the authors, and every excluded record whose abstract mentions pointing in a vision-language context.
Eighteen of the 85 records were candidates known to the authors, among them the general vision-language models.
Fifteen of the 85 records were included after this check.
Most of them were general vision-language models whose abstracts mention pointing as one capability among many (Qwen3-VL, Molmo, MiMo-Embodied, HY-Embodied, LLaVA-OneVision-2, Pelican-VL, Anvil, ACE-Brain).
Another one was SpatialPoint, whose abstract does not state that its depth map may come from an estimator.
At stage~2, language-model agents read the method and experiment sections where an open copy existed, and each agent recorded which source it read.
Each value of the classification is backed by a quotation or a section reference.
The extraction form of the method table has the fields granularity, pointer, generalisability, output, input and role of the point.
Of 1350 reports sought, 124 could not be retrieved or classified, and 1226 were assessed.
The 124 were counted as a separate category, not as excluded.
The second author verified 26 randomly drawn included rows without seeing the agents' decisions.
The second author agreed with 25 decisions and with all 208 compared values.
Thirty rows had been drawn for this check, and four of them could not be read for lack of access to the text.

\emph{Boundaries of the scope.}
Some robotics methods do not output a point but a path, that is, a sequence of positions that the gripper of a robot should pass through to carry out an instruction such as ``wipe the table'' or ``put the cup on the shelf''.
The path is a plan of motion, not a location of ``something in the scene'', so these methods do not locate a named point.
They are kept in the map and counted separately, like the one-category methods.
The reason is that the first or the last position of a path is often a named point, for example the handle to be grasped.
The methods are not designed to predict such a point, however.
Natively two-dimensional methods have no 3D scene.
Models for graphical interfaces, such as Aria-UI, UGround and SeeClick, output a pixel on a screenshot, and a screenshot shows no 3D scene.
Therefore these models were excluded.
Datasets (E7) and papers that do not describe a method (E9) were also excluded.
% TODO: E3 ``existing detector'' (233 records at stage 1, 39 at stage 2) is not defined here or in the paper; its definition is in the screening instruction of the review plan (ask Zofia).
The 3D boxes of general vision-language models are a separate output, not a 3D point.
A 3D box encloses the whole object, so it cannot distinguish points on the object.
Section~5 of the paper therefore combines the depth of the box centre with a predicted pixel, in the route called box depth.

\emph{Further limitations.}
Some records that came from the reference lists are fragments of a bibliography (an author name, a journal, a sentence) with no abstract.
Stage~2 excluded them as unidentifiable, and a few were excluded on the title alone.
Each cell of the method table records what the paper states.
Where the paper does not give a value, the cell reads ``n/s'' (not stated).
